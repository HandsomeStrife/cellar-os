How to demo CellarOS

A run-sheet: what to click, in what order, and the exact wines to search so the good bits actually show. Every figure on this page was read off production on 11 August 2026.

Before you open the laptop

Three things worth knowing so nothing surprises you mid-sentence.

  • Sign in at cellar-os.on-forge.com. Every demo account uses the password password.
  • Two finished areas are switched off and will 404: the sourcing map, and pricing/checkout. They are behind flags, not broken. Don't go looking for them in the nav.
  • Ask Dan for a reset before a live demo if someone has been clicking around. It takes about three seconds and puts the baskets, orders and stock back where this page says they are.

The run

Signed in as demo@cellaros.test. About twelve minutes at a walking pace. The order matters: the basket you build in step 4 becomes the purchase orders in step 5.

  1. Dashboard

    Open on the money

    Read the in-stock value and the low-stock alerts out loud. Two of the stock lines are deliberately down to a few bottles.

    Worth saying

    "This is what's in the cellar, and what it's worth, without anyone counting bottles."

  2. Catalogue

    Show the table, then filter to one merchant

    Point out the Producer and Supplier columns and the column picker. Filter to a single merchant and watch the Supplier column drop out of its own accord, because it has stopped telling you anything.

  3. Catalogue · search box

    Ask for a wine in English

    Type something fizzy and pink for a wedding, under £40. It fills in Type = Sparkling, Style = Sparkling Rosé and a £40 ceiling, then says why. The filters it set are the ordinary ones, still sitting there for you to correct.

    Worth saying

    "It isn't writing a database query. It's filling in the same filters you can see, so you can always tell what it understood."

  4. Catalogue · the + button

    The price comparison

    Search Fontclaire and press + on the £14.00 row. The panel opens with a quantity stepper, and underneath it the same wine from Ashgrove at £11.60. Press "Use this one" and the quantity comes with you.

    Worth saying

    "Same wine, same vintage, same bottle. £2.40 a bottle. Nobody was going to spot that across two PDFs."

  5. Basket

    Turn the basket into purchase orders

    Add a couple more wines from different merchants first. One button splits them into a purchase order per merchant, each ready to send.

  6. Orders

    Repeat the received order

    Find the order marked Received and repeat it. It comes back as a draft priced at today's catalogue, with a report of everything that moved since. It was placed at about 8% under current prices, so there is always something to show.

    Worth saying

    "Same again, at this week's prices, and it tells you what changed rather than quietly charging you last month's."

  7. Inventory

    Nudge a quantity

    Stock is per venue and carries the full wine detail, not just a name and a number. Same column picker as the catalogue.

  8. Suppliers → Ashgrove → Price lists

    A PDF becoming a catalogue

    Open the list waiting for review. This is the heart of it: the recipe it worked out on the left, the wines it read on the right, one row flagged for a human. Approve a row, reject one, and note that bulk approve skips anything flagged.

    Worth saying

    "A merchant sends a 200-page PDF. This is the review step, and after the first one for that merchant it's mostly free."

  9. The same review screen

    Teach it a merchant's vocabulary

    Two words are waiting to be mapped: Skin Contact and Vin de Voile. Map the first to Orange. It remembers for that merchant's next list.

  10. Sign in as group@cellaros.test

    Two minutes on the Group plan

    Two venues in the selector, each with its own merchants, stock and orders. The Team page shows a member scoped to Riverside only. Multiple venues is the entire difference between the two plans.

Prices a merchant won't quote

Search Dard et Ribo. Seven of the eight rows come back marked TBC rather than blank or missing. Les Caves de Pyrene get tiny allocations from this grower and print a word where the figure should go; the wine is still real and the buyer still needs to know it exists. There are eleven such wines in the catalogue.

Style, not just colour

Filter Type = Sparkling and a Style filter appears: 476 sparkling whites, 124 rosés, 21 pét-nats and 12 sparkling reds. Type = Fortified gives 23 sherries, 11 ports and 5 vermouths. All of it worked out from the wine names, on real merchant data.

Two things to know before someone else finds them

Both are in the real catalogue, so they are reachable from trade@. Neither is a reason not to demo; both are a reason to steer.

Haynes Hanson & Clark's prices are wrong

All 584 of their wines look like case prices recorded as bottle prices. Bâtard-Montrachet shows at £2,970 "a bottle" against a case of six, which is about £495 a bottle and roughly right. 63% of their list sits above £300 a bottle, against 0.3% of Les Caves de Pyrene's.

Don't use them for a price comparison. Their rows are what makes the Pol Roger Churchill comparison look absurd. Dan has this on the list.

There is a test merchant in the catalogue

"ZZZ Bulk Approve Test" holds 490 rows of generated nonsense — wines called molestias ut by a producer called Marquardt. It is left over from load-testing the bulk approve in July and it sorts to the bottom of most lists. Avoid sorting by merchant name in front of anyone.

The awkward questions

Is the AI writing database queries against our data?
No, and deliberately not. It reads the sentence into the same filter fields you can see on screen, and the search then runs through exactly the code a hand-set filter uses. It cannot reach another company's wines because it never gets to write the query. Whatever it understood is visible and correctable.
What does a search cost us?
About a fifth of a penny. It runs on Haiku, is cached for an hour per company and question, and every call is recorded in the ledger at /admin/costs.
Why do so few wines have a cross-merchant match?
Because the matching is strict on purpose: same producer, same wine, same vintage, same bottle size. Telling a buyer they are overpaying for a wine that turns out to be a different vintage is worse than saying nothing. These fifteen merchants overlap very little in practice, which is itself the honest answer about the trade.
How much does it cost to read a merchant's list?
The first time, a few pounds at most for a big one; a 215-page list came in around $5.70. After that the recipe is saved and re-reading the same merchant's next edition is usually free, because it never goes near the model again.
What happens when a merchant sends a scan?
It's detected and refused with a clear message rather than producing nonsense. There is a demo document sitting in exactly that state under Northbank in the supplier portal.
